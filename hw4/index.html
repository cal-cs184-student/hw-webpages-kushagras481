Part 1: Masses and springs

The cloth is represented as a grid of point masses connected by three types of springs, each encoding a different physical constraint. I built the grid in row-major (x-axis) order: for a cloth with num_width_points × num_height_points masses spanning a width × height region, each mass at grid index \((x, y)\) is placed at world position

\[ \mathbf{p}_{x,y} = \left(\frac{x}{N_w - 1} \cdot w,\; 1,\; \frac{y}{N_h - 1} \cdot h\right) \]

for a HORIZONTAL cloth (fixed \(y = 1\), varying over the \(xz\)-plane), and

\[ \mathbf{p}_{x,y} = \left(\frac{x}{N_w - 1} \cdot w,\; \frac{y}{N_h - 1} \cdot h,\; \delta_z\right) \]

for a VERTICAL cloth (varying over the \(xy\)-plane), where \(\delta_z \in [-0.001, 0.001]\) is a small random offset added to break the perfect planarity and prevent degeneracy during simulation.

Once all point masses are placed, springs are added to encode three constraint types:

  • Structural — connect each mass to its immediate left neighbor \((x-1, y)\) and the mass directly above it \((x, y-1)\). These resist compression and stretching along the cloth's primary axes.
  • Shearing — connect each mass to its diagonal upper-left \((x-1, y-1)\) and diagonal upper-right \((x+1, y-1)\) neighbors. These resist in-plane shear deformation.
  • Bending — connect each mass to the mass two steps to its left \((x-2, y)\) and two steps above \((x, y-2)\). These provide out-of-plane bending stiffness, acting over a longer range than structural springs.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

To animate the cloth, I implemented three steps inside Cloth::simulate:

Force accumulation

At the start of each timestep all per-mass forces vectors are reset. A single external force \(\mathbf{F}_\text{ext} = m \sum_i \mathbf{a}_i\) is computed from the external accelerations and applied uniformly to every point mass. Spring correction forces follow Hooke's law:

\[ \mathbf{F}_s = k_s \left(\|\mathbf{p}_b - \mathbf{p}_a\| - l_0\right) \cdot \frac{\mathbf{p}_b - \mathbf{p}_a}{\|\mathbf{p}_b - \mathbf{p}_a\|} \]

where \(l_0\) is the spring's rest length. The force is added to mass \(a\) and subtracted from mass \(b\) (equal and opposite). Bending springs use \(0.2 \cdot k_s\) so they are weaker than structural/shearing springs, matching the physical intuition that cloth resists stretching more than bending.

Verlet integration

New positions are computed with damped Verlet integration. Pinned masses are skipped. For every free mass:

\[ \mathbf{x}_{t+dt} = \mathbf{x}_t + (1 - d)\,(\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{x}_{t-dt}) + \frac{\mathbf{F}}{m}\,dt^2 \]

where \(d = \texttt{damping}/100\). The term \((1-d)(\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{x}_{t-dt})\) approximates \(v_t \cdot dt\) while bleeding off a small fraction of kinetic energy each step to model friction and heat loss.

Provot length constraint

After integration, each spring is checked: if its current length exceeds \(1.1 \cdot l_0\), the two endpoint masses are nudged toward each other along the spring direction to bring the length back to \(1.1 \cdot l_0\). The correction is split evenly between the two masses unless one is pinned, in which case the full correction is applied to the free mass.

Parameter experiments (scene/pinned2.json)

Spring constant \(k_s\)

Low \(k_s\) (50 N/m): With very weak springs, the cloth loses its structural rigidity. The sides bow strongly inward toward each other, the top edge curves down sharply, and the whole surface develops visible wrinkles and ripples at rest. The cloth looks almost rubbery — unable to hold its own shape against gravity.
High \(k_s\) (50,000 N/m): With very stiff springs, the cloth behaves almost like a rigid panel. It hangs nearly flat and planar between the two pinned corners with barely any visible curvature or droop — the spring forces are strong enough to almost completely counteract gravity.

Density

Low density (1 g/cm²): The cloth is so light that spring forces easily support it against gravity. At rest, the surface is nearly completely flat with almost no visible droop or curvature — it barely deforms from its initial shape.
High density (150 g/cm²): The heavier mass means each point mass exerts a much larger gravitational force on the springs. The sides bow outward and the bottom edge droops noticeably more than at default density. The effect looks similar to lowering \(k_s\), but the cause is different — the springs are equally stiff, there is just more weight pulling them down.

Damping

Low damping (~0%): Almost no kinetic energy is removed each timestep, so the cloth swings far past its equilibrium position and keeps oscillating. The screenshot shows the cloth mid-swing, rotated and twisted well past where it should rest — it behaves like cloth in a vacuum with no air resistance or friction.
High damping (1%): A large fraction of velocity is bled off each step, so the cloth descends in extreme slow motion. The screenshot was taken well into the simulation yet the cloth has barely moved from its initial flat position — it is still nearly vertical and planar, only just beginning to fall. It eventually settles without any oscillation.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Implementation

Sphere collisions

For each point mass, I check whether its current position lies inside the sphere (i.e. \(\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{o}\| \leq r\)). If so, I compute the tangent point — the point on the sphere's surface directly along the ray from the sphere's origin through the point mass's current position:

\[ \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{o} + r \cdot \frac{\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{o}}{\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{o}\|} \]

The correction vector brings last_position to the tangent point, and the new position is that correction scaled down by friction:

\[ \mathbf{p}_\text{new} = \mathbf{p}_\text{last} + (1 - f)\,(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}) \]

Plane collisions

A point mass crosses the plane when \(\text{sign}(\mathbf{d}_\text{last}) \neq \text{sign}(\mathbf{d}_\text{curr})\), where \(\mathbf{d} = (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{q}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is the signed distance from the plane's reference point \(\mathbf{q}\) along its normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}\). When a crossing is detected, I find the exact intersection by ray-casting from last_position toward position:

\[ t = \frac{(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}}{(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}}, \quad \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{p}_\text{last} + t\,(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}) \]

The target is nudged a small SURFACE_OFFSET back toward the original side of the plane, and the same friction-scaled correction is applied:

\[ \mathbf{p}_\text{new} = \mathbf{p}_\text{last} + (1 - f)\,\bigl(\mathbf{t} + \epsilon\,\text{side}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}\bigr) \]

In Cloth::simulate, after Verlet integration, every point mass is tested against every collision object by calling co->collide(pm) in a nested loop.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Implementation

Checking every pair of point masses for self-collision is \(O(n^2)\), which is too slow for real-time simulation. Instead, I implemented spatial hashing to bring this down to approximately \(O(n)\).

hash_position

The 3D space is partitioned into axis-aligned boxes of dimensions \(w \times h \times t\) where \(w = 3W/N_w\), \(h = 3H/N_h\), and \(t = \max(w, h)\). Each point mass's position is mapped to the integer coordinates of its enclosing box via \(\lfloor p_x/w \rfloor\), \(\lfloor p_y/h \rfloor\), \(\lfloor p_z/t \rfloor\), and those three integers are combined into a single float using large prime multipliers to minimize hash collisions:

\[ \text{hash} = \lfloor p_x/w \rfloor \cdot 92837111 + \lfloor p_y/h \rfloor \cdot 689287499 + \lfloor p_z/t \rfloor \cdot 283923481 \]

build_spatial_map

At the start of each timestep the map is cleared and rebuilt by iterating over every point mass, hashing its position, and inserting a pointer to it into the corresponding bucket. This takes \(O(n)\) time.

self_collide

For a given point mass, I look up its hash bucket and check each candidate in the bucket (excluding the mass itself). If the distance to a candidate is less than \(2 \cdot \texttt{thickness}\), I compute a repulsive correction vector that would push the mass far enough away to restore that minimum separation. The average of all pairwise corrections is then divided by simulation_steps to keep position updates gradual:

\[ \Delta\mathbf{p} = \frac{1}{n \cdot \text{steps}} \sum_i \hat{d}_i \,(2t - \|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_i\|) \]

where \(\hat{d}_i\) is the unit vector from candidate \(i\) toward the point mass. build_spatial_map and self_collide are called inside Cloth::simulate after Verlet integration and before the Provot length constraint.

Effect of varying density

Low density (1 g/cm²): The cloth is light so spring forces dominate over gravity. It falls slowly and settles into just 2–3 wide, gentle pleats spread broadly across the plane — barely crumpled, almost flat.
High density (150 g/cm²): Gravity dominates and the cloth falls faster and harder. It crumples into a more compact pile with tighter, more numerous folds packed into a smaller area on the ground.

Low \(k_s\) (500 N/m): The weak springs offer little resistance to bending, so the cloth folds loosely with many overlapping layers spread across the ground. The folds are numerous but relatively shallow.
High \(k_s\) (50,000 N/m): The stiff springs strongly resist bending, so the cloth maintains its structure as it falls. It settles into very few, clean, broad folds — almost like a folded board — forming a thick, structured pile rather than a crumpled heap.

Part 5: Shaders

What is a shader program?

A shader program is a small, isolated program that runs entirely on the GPU in parallel across many threads simultaneously — one thread per vertex or fragment. Unlike CPU code, each shader invocation has no visibility into what other invocations are doing; it just reads its inputs and writes a single output. This parallelism is what makes real-time rendering possible.

A full shader pipeline pairs two shader types: a vertex shader and a fragment shader. The vertex shader runs once per vertex and is responsible for transforming geometry — it converts model-space positions and normals into world space and clips them to screen space, writing the final screen-space position to gl_Position. Any extra per-vertex data (world-space position, normal, UV coordinates) is passed along as varying outputs. After the vertex shader, the GPU rasterizes the triangles and interpolates those varyings across the surface using barycentric coordinates. The fragment shader then runs once per rasterized fragment, receiving the interpolated varyings and computing a final color. Together they implement a full lighting and material model: the vertex shader sets up the geometry, the fragment shader evaluates the shading equation.

Blinn-Phong shading model

Blinn-Phong decomposes light into three components:

  • Ambient \(k_a I_a\) — a constant base color that approximates indirect, bounced light. It prevents surfaces facing away from the light from going completely black.
  • Diffuse \(k_d (I/r^2) \max(0, \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})\) — Lambertian scattering that depends only on the angle between the surface normal \(\mathbf{n}\) and the light direction \(\mathbf{l}\). It is view-independent.
  • Specular \(k_s (I/r^2) \max(0, \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{h})^p\) — a shiny highlight that depends on the half-vector \(\mathbf{h} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v})\) between the light and view directions. The exponent \(p\) controls the tightness of the highlight.

My values: \(k_a = 0.1\), \(k_d = 0.8\), \(k_s = 0.5\), \(I_a = (1,1,1)\), \(p = 64\).

Texture mapping

The texture shader simply samples u_texture_1 at the interpolated UV coordinate v_uv using GLSL's built-in texture() function and outputs that color directly.

Bump and displacement mapping

Both shaders use the same height map \(h(u,v)\) = the red channel of u_texture_2 sampled at the fragment's UV coordinates.

Bump mapping only modifies the shading normal — geometry is unchanged. Finite differences give the height gradient:

\[ dU = (h(u + 1/w,\, v) - h(u,v)) \cdot k_h k_n, \quad dV = (h(u,\, v + 1/h) - h(u,v)) \cdot k_h k_n \]

The local-space normal \(\mathbf{n}_o = (-dU, -dV, 1)\) is transformed to world space via the TBN matrix \([{\bf t},\, {\bf b},\, {\bf n}]\) where \({\bf b} = {\bf n} \times {\bf t}\). This perturbed normal is then used in Blinn-Phong shading.

Displacement mapping goes further — it actually moves vertices along their model-space normal in the vertex shader: \(\mathbf{p}' = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{n} \cdot h(u,v) \cdot k_h\). The fragment shader is identical to bump mapping.

Bump mapping only perturbs normals — the sphere silhouette stays perfectly smooth no matter what. Displacement mapping physically moves vertices, so at larger height values the silhouette would visibly deform. At Height=0.1 both shaders look nearly identical, but the coarseness comparison below reveals the key difference: bump mapping is resolution-independent while displacement quality scales directly with vertex density.

Mirror shader

The mirror shader computes the outgoing eye ray \(\mathbf{w}_o = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c})\), reflects it across the surface normal to get the incoming direction \(\mathbf{w}_i = \text{reflect}(\mathbf{w}_o, \mathbf{n})\), and samples the cubemap environment texture in that direction using texture(u_texture_cubemap, wi).
