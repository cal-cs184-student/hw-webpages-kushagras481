Overview

In this homework I built a real-time cloth simulator from scratch. I started with a grid of point masses connected by structural, shearing, and bending springs, then implemented damped Verlet integration to animate the cloth under gravity, with a Provot length constraint to keep springs from over-stretching. From there I added collision response against spheres and planes, plus self-collision detection via spatial hashing so the cloth doesn't clip through itself. Finally, I wrote a bunch of GLSL shaders — diffuse, Blinn-Phong, texture mapping, bump and displacement mapping, and a mirror reflection shader using a cubemap. Honestly the most satisfying part was seeing how much realistic behavior you can get from such simple physics: a few spring types and one integrator produce cloth that actually looks and feels right, and tricks like bump mapping show how much you can fake with just a normal perturbation and no extra geometry.

Part 1: Masses and springs

The cloth is represented as a grid of point masses connected by three types of springs. I built the grid in row-major order: for a cloth with num_width_points × num_height_points masses spanning a width × height region, each mass at index \((x, y)\) is placed at

\[ \mathbf{p}_{x,y} = \left(\frac{x}{N_w - 1} \cdot w,\; 1,\; \frac{y}{N_h - 1} \cdot h\right) \]

for a HORIZONTAL cloth (fixed \(y = 1\), laid flat in the \(xz\)-plane), and

\[ \mathbf{p}_{x,y} = \left(\frac{x}{N_w - 1} \cdot w,\; \frac{y}{N_h - 1} \cdot h,\; \delta_z\right) \]

for a VERTICAL cloth (hanging in the \(xy\)-plane), where \(\delta_z \in [-0.001, 0.001]\) is a tiny random z-offset to break perfect planarity so the simulation doesn't degenerate.

After placing all masses, I added three types of springs:

  • Structural — connect each mass to its immediate left \((x-1, y)\) and upper \((x, y-1)\) neighbors. These resist stretching along the cloth's main axes.
  • Shearing — connect each mass diagonally to \((x-1, y-1)\) and \((x+1, y-1)\). These resist in-plane shear.
  • Bending — connect each mass to the mass two steps left \((x-2, y)\) and two steps up \((x, y-2)\). These resist out-of-plane bending over a longer range than structural springs.

Part 2: Simulation via numerical integration

To actually animate the cloth, I implemented three steps inside Cloth::simulate:

Force accumulation

At the start of each timestep all per-mass forces vectors are zeroed out. I compute a single external force \(\mathbf{F}_\text{ext} = m \sum_i \mathbf{a}_i\) from the external accelerations and apply it uniformly to every point mass. Then spring forces follow Hooke's law:

\[ \mathbf{F}_s = k_s \left(\|\mathbf{p}_b - \mathbf{p}_a\| - l_0\right) \cdot \frac{\mathbf{p}_b - \mathbf{p}_a}{\|\mathbf{p}_b - \mathbf{p}_a\|} \]

where \(l_0\) is the rest length. The force is added to mass \(a\) and subtracted from mass \(b\). Bending springs use \(0.2 \cdot k_s\) to make them weaker — cloth resists stretching much more than bending.

Verlet integration

New positions are computed with damped Verlet integration. Pinned masses are skipped. For every free mass:

\[ \mathbf{x}_{t+dt} = \mathbf{x}_t + (1 - d)\,(\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{x}_{t-dt}) + \frac{\mathbf{F}}{m}\,dt^2 \]

where \(d = \texttt{damping}/100\). The \((1-d)(\mathbf{x}_t - \mathbf{x}_{t-dt})\) term is basically velocity times dt with a fraction bled off each step to model friction and energy loss.

Provot length constraint

After integration, I check every spring: if it's stretched beyond \(1.1 \times l_0\), I push the two endpoint masses toward each other to bring it back to that limit. If one mass is pinned, the full correction goes to the free one; otherwise it's split evenly.

Parameter experiments (scene/pinned2.json)

Spring constant \(k_s\)

Low \(k_s\) (50 N/m): From the very start, the cloth falls quickly and oscillates a lot — weak springs can't pull it back to equilibrium effectively, so it swings past where it should stop and keeps bouncing for a long time before finally settling. At rest, the sides bow strongly inward, the top edge curves sharply down, and the entire surface is covered in wrinkles. It basically can't hold its own shape against gravity.
High \(k_s\) (50,000 N/m): With stiff springs, the cloth behaves almost like a rigid panel from the start — it falls without much oscillation, barely sways, and settles quickly. At rest, it hangs nearly flat between the two pinned corners with barely any droop. The spring forces are strong enough to almost fully counteract gravity.

Density

Low density (1 g/cm²): The cloth is so light that the springs easily support it during the entire fall — it descends slowly, barely oscillates, and settles very quickly into its resting shape. At rest the surface is almost completely flat with barely any droop. Gravity hardly affects it at all.
High density (150 g/cm²): The heavier mass means gravity pulls much harder at every step. The cloth falls faster, hits equilibrium with more momentum, and oscillates noticeably more before settling. At rest, the sides bow outward and the bottom droops significantly more than at default density. It looks similar to low \(k_s\) but the cause is different — the springs are just as stiff, there's just more weight pulling on them.

Damping

Low damping (~0%): Almost no energy is removed each step, so the cloth swings way past its equilibrium and keeps oscillating. The screenshot shows it mid-swing, rotated and twisted far past where it should settle — like cloth in a vacuum with no air resistance.
High damping (1%): A big chunk of velocity gets removed each step, so the cloth descends in extremely slow motion. The screenshot was taken well into the simulation and the cloth has barely moved from its starting position — it's still nearly vertical and flat, just beginning to fall.

Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

Implementation

Sphere collisions

For each point mass, I check if it's inside the sphere (\(\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{o}\| \leq r\)). If so, I find the tangent point — the point on the sphere's surface along the ray from the origin through the mass's current position:

\[ \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{o} + r \cdot \frac{\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{o}}{\|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{o}\|} \]

Then I compute a correction from last_position to the tangent point and scale it down by friction to get the new position:

\[ \mathbf{p}_\text{new} = \mathbf{p}_\text{last} + (1 - f)\,(\mathbf{t} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}) \]

Plane collisions

A point mass crosses the plane when \(\text{sign}(\mathbf{d}_\text{last}) \neq \text{sign}(\mathbf{d}_\text{curr})\), where \(\mathbf{d} = (\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{q}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}\) is the signed distance from the plane. When a crossing is detected, I find the exact intersection by ray-casting from last_position toward position:

\[ t = \frac{(\mathbf{q} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}}{(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}) \cdot \hat{\mathbf{n}}}, \quad \mathbf{t} = \mathbf{p}_\text{last} + t\,(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}) \]

The target gets nudged a small SURFACE_OFFSET back toward the original side so the cloth doesn't z-fight with the plane surface, and then the same friction-scaled correction is applied:

\[ \mathbf{p}_\text{new} = \mathbf{p}_\text{last} + (1 - f)\,\bigl(\mathbf{t} + \epsilon\,\text{side}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}} - \mathbf{p}_\text{last}\bigr) \]

Inside Cloth::simulate, after Verlet integration, every point mass is tested against every collision object via co->collide(pm).

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Implementation

Naively checking every pair of point masses for self-collision is \(O(n^2)\) — way too slow for real-time. Instead I used spatial hashing to get it down to roughly \(O(n)\).

hash_position

I partition 3D space into axis-aligned boxes of dimensions \(w \times h \times t\) where \(w = 3W/N_w\), \(h = 3H/N_h\), and \(t = \max(w, h)\). Each point mass maps to the integer coordinates of its box via \(\lfloor p_x/w \rfloor\), \(\lfloor p_y/h \rfloor\), \(\lfloor p_z/t \rfloor\), and those three integers get combined into one float using large primes to minimize collisions:

\[ \text{hash} = \lfloor p_x/w \rfloor \cdot 92837111 + \lfloor p_y/h \rfloor \cdot 689287499 + \lfloor p_z/t \rfloor \cdot 283923481 \]

build_spatial_map

At the start of each timestep the map is cleared and rebuilt by iterating over every mass, hashing its position, and inserting a pointer to it into the corresponding bucket. \(O(n)\) total.

self_collide

For a given point mass, I look up its bucket and check each candidate (skipping itself). If the distance to a candidate is less than \(2 \times \texttt{thickness}\), I compute a repulsive correction that pushes the mass far enough away to restore minimum separation. The average correction across all nearby candidates is divided by simulation_steps to keep things gradual:

\[ \Delta\mathbf{p} = \frac{1}{n \cdot \text{steps}} \sum_i \hat{d}_i \,(2t - \|\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{p}_i\|) \]

where \(\hat{d}_i\) is the unit vector from candidate \(i\) toward the mass. Both build_spatial_map and self_collide are called after Verlet integration and before the Provot constraint.

Part 5: Shaders

What is a shader program?

A shader program is a small program that runs entirely on the GPU, executing in parallel across many threads at once — one per vertex or fragment. Each invocation is isolated: it just reads its inputs and writes one output, with no idea what other threads are doing. This parallelism is what makes real-time rendering feasible.

A full shader pipeline pairs two stages. The vertex shader runs once per vertex and handles geometry — it transforms positions and normals from model space into world space and writes the final screen-space position to gl_Position. Any extra per-vertex data (world position, normal, UV) gets passed along as varying outputs. The GPU then rasterizes the triangles and interpolates those varyings across the surface using barycentric coordinates. The fragment shader runs once per rasterized pixel, takes those interpolated varyings, and computes the final color. The vertex shader sets up the geometry; the fragment shader does the actual shading.

Blinn-Phong shading model

Blinn-Phong breaks lighting into three components:

  • Ambient \(k_a I_a\) — a constant base color that approximates indirect light bouncing around the scene. Keeps surfaces from going fully black even when they face away from the light.
  • Diffuse \(k_d (I/r^2) \max(0, \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{l})\) — Lambertian scattering based on the angle between the surface normal \(\mathbf{n}\) and light direction \(\mathbf{l}\). View-independent.
  • Specular \(k_s (I/r^2) \max(0, \mathbf{n} \cdot \mathbf{h})^p\) — the shiny highlight, driven by the half-vector \(\mathbf{h} = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{l} + \mathbf{v})\) between light and view. The exponent \(p\) controls how tight/narrow the highlight is.

I used \(k_a = 0.1\), \(k_d = 0.8\), \(k_s = 0.5\), \(I_a = (1,1,1)\), \(p = 64\).

Texture mapping

The texture shader just samples u_texture_1 at the interpolated UV coordinate v_uv using GLSL's texture() and outputs that color directly. No lighting math at all.

Bump and displacement mapping

Both shaders use the same height map \(h(u,v)\) — the red channel of u_texture_2 sampled at the fragment's UV. For all bump and displacement renders I used texture_3.png (not the default texture_2.png).

Bump mapping only touches the shading normal — no geometry is moved. I use finite differences to get the height gradient:

\[ dU = (h(u + 1/w,\, v) - h(u,v)) \cdot k_h k_n, \quad dV = (h(u,\, v + 1/h) - h(u,v)) \cdot k_h k_n \]

The local-space normal \(\mathbf{n}_o = (-dU, -dV, 1)\) is transformed to world space via the TBN matrix \([{\bf t},\, {\bf b},\, {\bf n}]\) where \({\bf b} = {\bf n} \times {\bf t}\). This perturbed normal goes into Blinn-Phong shading.

Displacement mapping goes one step further — it actually moves the vertices in the vertex shader: \(\mathbf{p}' = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{n} \cdot h(u,v) \cdot k_h\). The fragment shader is otherwise identical to bump mapping.

The key conceptual difference: bump mapping only perturbs normals so the silhouette is always perfectly smooth no matter what. Displacement mapping physically moves vertices, so at larger height values the silhouette visibly deforms to match the height map. At Height=0.1 both look nearly identical, but increasing the height would make displacement's geometry changes very obvious while bump would stay smooth on the outline regardless.

Effect of mesh coarseness on bump vs. displacement

This is where the real structural difference shows up. Bump mapping works entirely in the fragment shader and never touches vertices, so resolution has essentially no impact — a coarse 16×16 sphere and a dense 128×128 sphere produce nearly identical bump results because normal perturbation happens per-fragment regardless of vertex count. Displacement mapping moves actual vertices in the vertex shader, so it's completely bottlenecked by vertex density. With only 16×16 vertices, there aren't enough sample points to represent the height map faithfully — fine details get smeared out and you'd see blocky, jagged silhouettes at larger height values. With 128×128 vertices the dense grid can follow the height map closely, producing smoother and more detailed surface deformation. At Height=0.1 both coarseness levels look similar because the displacement is subtle, but the fundamental difference is clear: bump doesn't care about resolution, displacement absolutely does.

Mirror shader

The mirror shader computes the outgoing eye ray \(\mathbf{w}_o = \text{normalize}(\mathbf{p} - \mathbf{c})\), reflects it across the surface normal to get the incoming direction \(\mathbf{w}_i = \text{reflect}(\mathbf{w}_o, \mathbf{n})\), and samples the environment cubemap in that direction with texture(u_texture_cubemap, wi). Simple but surprisingly convincing.
