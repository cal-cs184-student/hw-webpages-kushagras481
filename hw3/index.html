Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray Generation and Primitive Intersection Pipeline

Ray generation begins in Camera::generate_ray(x, y), where normalized image coordinates \((x, y) \in [0,1]^2\) are mapped to a point on a virtual sensor plane at \(Z = -1\) in camera space. The bottom-left corner of this sensor is at \((-\tan(\text{hFov}/2),\, -\tan(\text{vFov}/2),\, -1)\) and the top-right corner is at \((\tan(\text{hFov}/2),\, \tan(\text{vFov}/2),\, -1)\), so the sensor point is: \[ \mathbf{s} = \bigl((2x-1)\tan(\text{hFov}/2),\; (2y-1)\tan(\text{vFov}/2),\; -1\bigr) \] This direction is transformed to world space using the camera-to-world matrix c2w, normalized, and the resulting ray originates at the camera position pos with min_t = nClip and max_t = fClip.

In PathTracer::raytrace_pixel(x, y), I estimate the radiance integral over a pixel using Monte Carlo sampling. For each of ns_aa samples, a random 2D offset is drawn from a uniform grid sampler and added to the pixel's bottom-left corner coordinates. These are normalized to \([0,1]^2\) and passed to generate_ray. The returned radiance values are averaged to produce the final pixel color, which is written into the sample buffer.

Once a ray is generated, it is tested for intersection against all scene primitives. For triangles, I use the Möller-Trumbore algorithm described below. For spheres, I solve the quadratic \(\|\mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d} - \mathbf{c}\|^2 = r^2\) for \(t\), taking the smaller valid root in \([\text{min\_t}, \text{max\_t}]\), with the surface normal computed as \((\mathbf{hit\_p} - \mathbf{c}) / r\). If any intersection is found, est_radiance_global_illumination shades the hit point by visualizing the surface normal as an RGB color.

Triangle Intersection: Möller–Trumbore Algorithm

Triangle intersection is implemented using the Möller–Trumbore algorithm, which solves for the intersection point directly in terms of barycentric coordinates without first computing the plane equation. Given a ray \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}\) and triangle vertices \(\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2, \mathbf{p}_3\), I compute: \[ \mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{p}_2 - \mathbf{p}_1, \quad \mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{p}_3 - \mathbf{p}_1, \quad \mathbf{s} = \mathbf{o} - \mathbf{p}_1 \] \[ \mathbf{s}_1 = \mathbf{d} \times \mathbf{e}_2, \quad \mathbf{s}_2 = \mathbf{s} \times \mathbf{e}_1 \] \[ t = \frac{\mathbf{s}_2 \cdot \mathbf{e}_2}{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{e}_1}, \quad b_1 = \frac{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{s}}{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{e}_1}, \quad b_2 = \frac{\mathbf{s}_2 \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{e}_1} \] The intersection is valid when \(b_1 \geq 0\), \(b_2 \geq 0\), \(b_1 + b_2 \leq 1\), and \(t \in [\text{min\_t},\, \text{max\_t}]\). The third barycentric coordinate is \(b_0 = 1 - b_1 - b_2\). The surface normal at the intersection is computed by interpolating the three vertex normals: \(\mathbf{n} = b_0 \mathbf{n}_1 + b_1 \mathbf{n}_2 + b_2 \mathbf{n}_3\), then normalized. The ray's max_t is updated to \(t\) so that subsequent intersections farther away are culled.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH Construction Algorithm

The BVH is constructed recursively in BVHAccel::construct_bvh. First, the bounding box over all primitives in the current range is computed by expanding an initially empty BBox with each primitive's bounding box. A new BVHNode is initialized with this bounding box.

If the number of primitives is at most max_leaf_size, the node is a leaf and its start and end iterators are set directly. Otherwise, I choose a split axis and partition the primitives recursively into left and right children.

Splitting heuristic: I pick the axis with the largest extent of the bounding box (the longest dimension). The split point is the average of all primitive centroids along that axis. Primitives whose centroid falls below the split point go to the left child; the rest go to the right. This heuristic keeps both halves spatially compact and roughly balanced. If the partition is degenerate and all primitives end up on one side, I fall back to splitting at the median index to prevent infinite recursion.

Large Scene Renders with BVH Acceleration

BVH Acceleration: Timing Comparison

The BVH provides a massive speedup over brute-force intersection. Without it, every ray must test against every primitive, an \(O(n)\) operation that makes complex scenes completely impractical. With BVH, traversal is \(O(\log n)\) because a ray that misses a node's bounding box skips all primitives in that subtree at once. In practice: cow.dae (5,856 triangles) rendered in 0.042 seconds with BVH versus roughly 40 seconds without, a ~950x speedup. maxplanck.dae (50,801 triangles) finished in 0.026 seconds and CBlucy.dae (133,796 triangles) in 0.024 seconds, scenes that would have been completely unusable without acceleration. The average intersection tests per ray dropped from hundreds to single digits across all scenes tested, confirming that the longest-axis centroid-split heuristic produces well-balanced trees.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Uniform Hemisphere Sampling

In estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere, I estimate direct lighting by sampling num_samples directions uniformly across the hemisphere around the hit point. For each sample direction \(\omega_i\) (in object space), I transform it to world space and cast a shadow ray from hit_p with min_t = EPS_F to avoid self-intersection. If this ray hits a surface with nonzero emission (a light), I accumulate the contribution using the Monte Carlo estimator of the rendering equation: \[ L_{\text{out}} \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{f(\omega_o, \omega_i) \cdot L_i \cdot \cos\theta_i}{p(\omega_i)} \] where \(p(\omega_i) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\) for uniform hemisphere sampling and \(\cos\theta_i = \omega_i.z\) since the surface normal is aligned with \(z\) in object space.

Importance Sampling Lights

In estimate_direct_lighting_importance, instead of sampling random directions, I sample directly from each light source. For each light, I call sample_L to get a sampled direction \(\omega_i\) in world space, the distance to the light, and the PDF. I transform \(\omega_i\) to object space and skip it if \(\cos\theta \leq 0\) since that means the light is behind the surface. Then I cast a shadow ray with max_t = distToLight - EPS_F to check for occlusion. If the path is unblocked, I accumulate: \[ L_{\text{out}} \mathrel{+}= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{f(\omega_o, \omega_j) \cdot L_j \cdot \cos\theta_j}{p(\omega_j)} \] For delta lights (point lights), only one sample is taken since all samples would be identical. For area lights, ns_area_light samples are used.

Hemisphere vs. Importance Sampling

Noise Comparison: Varying Light Rays (Importance Sampling, 1 sample/pixel)

With 1 light ray, soft shadows are extremely noisy because each pixel samples the area light only once, so whether a point is in shadow or not is nearly binary and random. Increasing to 4 rays reduces the noise substantially, and at 16 rays the shadow edges begin to look smooth. At 64 rays, the soft shadows are visually clean and the penumbra transitions are well-resolved, demonstrating that more light samples directly reduce variance in the shadow estimate.

Analysis: Hemisphere vs. Importance Sampling

Both methods converge to the same correct result, but importance sampling gets there much faster. With hemisphere sampling, the probability of a random direction actually hitting the area light is tiny (proportional to the light's solid angle), so most samples contribute zero radiance and the estimator has high variance. With importance sampling, every sample is guaranteed to point toward a light source, which drastically cuts down variance for the same sample count. You can clearly see this in the renders: the importance-sampled image has much smoother shading and softer shadows at the same settings. Another limitation of hemisphere sampling is that it cannot render point lights at all since the probability of randomly hitting a single point is zero, while importance sampling handles them naturally by sampling exactly once.

Part 4: Global Illumination

How Indirect Lighting is Computed

Global illumination is implemented in at_least_one_bounce_radiance, which recursively accumulates light contributions across multiple bounces. When called with a ray and intersection, the function returns immediately if r.depth == 0 (m=0, no bounces). Otherwise it computes one-bounce direct lighting via one_bounce_radiance, added unconditionally when isAccumBounces is true, or only at the deepest level (r.depth == 1) when false so that unaccumulated renders isolate exactly one specific bounce.

To continue the path, I sample a new direction using isect.bsdf->sample_f (cosine-weighted hemisphere), construct a new ray from the hit point with min_t = EPS_F and depth = r.depth - 1, and recursively call at_least_one_bounce_radiance if the new ray hits something. The indirect contribution is: \[ L_{\text{indirect}} = \frac{f(\omega_o, \omega_i) \cdot L_{\text{recursive}} \cdot \cos\theta}{p(\omega_i) \cdot p_{\text{RR}}} \] Russian Roulette is applied for all bounces after the first indirect one. With continuation probability \(p_{\text{RR}} = 0.65\), each path is randomly terminated with probability 0.35. The first indirect bounce always proceeds without RR, guaranteeing at least one indirect contribution when max_ray_depth > 1. The raytrace_pixel function initializes each camera ray's depth to max_ray_depth.

Part 5: Adaptive Sampling

How Adaptive Sampling Works

Uniform sampling traces a fixed number of rays through every pixel regardless of how simple or complex it is, which wastes a lot of compute on easy flat areas. Adaptive sampling fixes this by monitoring each pixel's radiance estimates as they come in and stopping early once the pixel has statistically converged. The check uses a 95% confidence interval: after n samples with running illuminance sum \(s_1 = \sum x_k\) and sum of squares \(s_2 = \sum x_k^2\), the mean and variance are \[ \mu = \frac{s_1}{n}, \qquad \sigma^2 = \frac{1}{n-1}\!\left(s_2 - \frac{s_1^2}{n}\right) \] and the convergence measure is \[ I = 1.96 \cdot \frac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}} \] If \(I \leq \texttt{maxTolerance} \cdot \mu\) (default tolerance = 0.05), the pixel is done. Otherwise sampling continues up to the maximum ns_aa. This way, easy pixels like flat walls and black backgrounds finish quickly, while complex areas like object edges and contact shadows get the samples they actually need.

Implementation

All the changes live in raytrace_pixel. I added two running scalars s1 and s2 that accumulate the per-sample illuminance \(x_k = \texttt{r.illum()}\) and \(x_k^2\). Every samplesPerBatch samples (default 32) I run the convergence check. If the pixel passes, the loop breaks early and the actual sample count n (not the maximum ns_aa) gets stored in sampleCountBuffer so the rate image reflects true usage. The final radiance is divided by n rather than ns_aa to keep the estimator unbiased.

Results (2048 samples/pixel max, -l 1, -m 5)

Both scenes were rendered with 2048 samples per pixel maximum, 1 light ray (-l 1), and max ray depth 5 (-m 5). The sampling rate images show exactly what I expected.
