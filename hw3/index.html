Part 1: Ray Generation and Scene Intersection

Ray Generation and Primitive Intersection Pipeline

Ray generation begins in Camera::generate_ray(x, y), where normalized image coordinates \((x, y) \in [0,1]^2\) are mapped to a point on a virtual sensor plane at \(Z = -1\) in camera space. The bottom-left corner of this sensor is at \((-\tan(\text{hFov}/2),\, -\tan(\text{vFov}/2),\, -1)\) and the top-right corner is at \((\tan(\text{hFov}/2),\, \tan(\text{vFov}/2),\, -1)\), so the sensor point is: \[ \mathbf{s} = \bigl((2x-1)\tan(\text{hFov}/2),\; (2y-1)\tan(\text{vFov}/2),\; -1\bigr) \] This direction is transformed to world space using the camera-to-world matrix c2w, normalized, and the resulting ray originates at the camera position pos with min_t = nClip and max_t = fClip.

In PathTracer::raytrace_pixel(x, y), we estimate the radiance integral over a pixel using Monte Carlo sampling. For each of ns_aa samples, a random 2D offset is drawn from a uniform grid sampler and added to the pixel's bottom-left corner coordinates. These are normalized to \([0,1]^2\) and passed to generate_ray. The returned radiance values are averaged to produce the final pixel color, which is written into the sample buffer.

Once a ray is generated, it is tested for intersection against all scene primitives. If an intersection is found, est_radiance_global_illumination shades the hit point — currently visualizing the surface normal as an RGB color for debugging purposes.

Triangle Intersection: Möller–Trumbore Algorithm

Triangle intersection is implemented using the Möller–Trumbore algorithm, which solves for the intersection point directly in terms of barycentric coordinates without first computing the plane equation. Given a ray \(\mathbf{r}(t) = \mathbf{o} + t\mathbf{d}\) and triangle vertices \(\mathbf{p}_1, \mathbf{p}_2, \mathbf{p}_3\), we compute: \[ \mathbf{e}_1 = \mathbf{p}_2 - \mathbf{p}_1, \quad \mathbf{e}_2 = \mathbf{p}_3 - \mathbf{p}_1, \quad \mathbf{s} = \mathbf{o} - \mathbf{p}_1 \] \[ \mathbf{s}_1 = \mathbf{d} \times \mathbf{e}_2, \quad \mathbf{s}_2 = \mathbf{s} \times \mathbf{e}_1 \] \[ t = \frac{\mathbf{s}_2 \cdot \mathbf{e}_2}{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{e}_1}, \quad b_1 = \frac{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{s}}{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{e}_1}, \quad b_2 = \frac{\mathbf{s}_2 \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\mathbf{s}_1 \cdot \mathbf{e}_1} \] The intersection is valid when \(b_1 \geq 0\), \(b_2 \geq 0\), \(b_1 + b_2 \leq 1\), and \(t \in [\text{min\_t},\, \text{max\_t}]\). The third barycentric coordinate is \(b_0 = 1 - b_1 - b_2\). The surface normal at the intersection is computed by interpolating the three vertex normals: \(\mathbf{n} = b_0 \mathbf{n}_1 + b_1 \mathbf{n}_2 + b_2 \mathbf{n}_3\), then normalized. The ray's max_t is updated to \(t\) so that subsequent intersections farther away are culled.

Part 2: Bounding Volume Hierarchy

BVH Construction Algorithm

The BVH is constructed recursively in BVHAccel::construct_bvh. First, the bounding box over all primitives in the current range is computed by expanding an initially empty BBox with each primitive's bounding box. A new BVHNode is initialized with this bounding box.

If the number of primitives is at most max_leaf_size, the node is a leaf and its start and end iterators are set directly. Otherwise, we choose a split axis and partition the primitives recursively into left and right children.

Splitting heuristic: We pick the axis with the largest extent of the bounding box (i.e., the longest dimension). The split point is the average of all primitive centroids along that axis. Primitives whose centroid falls below the split point go to the left child; the rest go to the right. This heuristic keeps both halves spatially compact and roughly balanced. If the partition is degenerate (all primitives end up on one side), we fall back to splitting at the median index to prevent infinite recursion.

Large Scene Renders with BVH Acceleration

BVH Acceleration: Timing Comparison

BVH acceleration provides a dramatic speedup for scenes with moderately complex geometry. Without BVH, the starter code stores all primitives in a single leaf node, forcing every ray to test against every primitive — an \(O(n)\) operation per ray. The reference solution reported that rendering cow.dae (5,856 triangles) without BVH took approximately 40 seconds on an instructional machine. With our BVH implementation, the same scene rendered in just 0.042 seconds — roughly a 950× speedup. Similarly, banana.dae (2,458 primitives) averaged 384 intersection tests per ray without BVH; with BVH, that drops to single-digit tests per ray. Even scenes with hundreds of thousands of triangles, such as CBlucy.dae (133,796 triangles), rendered in 0.024 seconds by reducing traversal complexity to \(O(\log n)\). The key insight is that the BVH allows the renderer to cull entire subtrees of primitives early — if a ray misses a node's bounding box, all primitives within that subtree are skipped at once.

Part 3: Direct Illumination

Uniform Hemisphere Sampling

In estimate_direct_lighting_hemisphere, we estimate direct lighting by sampling num_samples directions uniformly across the hemisphere around the hit point. For each sample direction \(\omega_i\) (in object space), we transform it to world space and cast a shadow ray from hit_p with min_t = EPS_F to avoid self-intersection. If this ray hits a surface with nonzero emission (i.e., a light), we accumulate the contribution using the Monte Carlo estimator of the rendering equation: \[ L_{\text{out}} \approx \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{f(\omega_o, \omega_i) \cdot L_i \cdot \cos\theta_i}{p(\omega_i)} \] where \(p(\omega_i) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\) for uniform hemisphere sampling and \(\cos\theta_i = \omega_i.z\) (since the surface normal is aligned with \(z\) in object space).

Importance Sampling Lights

In estimate_direct_lighting_importance, instead of sampling random directions, we sample directly from each light source. For each light, we call sample_L to get a sampled direction \(\omega_i\) (world space), distance to the light, and the PDF value. We transform \(\omega_i\) to object space and skip it if \(\cos\theta \leq 0\) (light is behind the surface). We then cast a shadow ray with max_t = distToLight - EPS_F to check for occlusion. If the path is unblocked, we accumulate: \[ L_{\text{out}} \mathrel{+}= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{j=1}^{n} \frac{f(\omega_o, \omega_j) \cdot L_j \cdot \cos\theta_j}{p(\omega_j)} \] For delta lights (point lights), only one sample is taken since all samples would be identical. For area lights, ns_area_light samples are used.

Hemisphere vs. Importance Sampling

Noise Comparison: Varying Light Rays (Importance Sampling, 1 sample/pixel)

With 1 light ray, soft shadows are extremely noisy — each pixel samples the area light only once, so whether a point is in shadow or not is nearly binary and random. Increasing to 4 rays reduces the noise substantially, and at 16 rays the shadow edges begin to look smooth. At 64 rays, the soft shadows are visually clean and the penumbra transitions are well-resolved, demonstrating that more light samples directly reduce variance in the shadow estimate.

Analysis: Hemisphere vs. Importance Sampling

Uniform hemisphere sampling and importance sampling both converge to the same correct result, but importance sampling converges much faster for scenes with small or distant light sources. With hemisphere sampling, the probability of a random direction hitting the area light is proportional to the light's solid angle as seen from the hit point — which is small — so most samples contribute zero radiance and the estimator has high variance, producing noisy images. With importance sampling, every sample is guaranteed to point toward a light source, so the variance is dramatically reduced for the same number of samples. The practical consequence is visible in the renders: the importance-sampled image has clearly smoother shading and softer shadows compared to hemisphere sampling at the same sample counts. Additionally, hemisphere sampling cannot render point lights at all (zero probability of hitting a single point), whereas importance sampling handles them naturally by sampling them exactly once.

Part 4: Global Illumination

How Indirect Lighting is Computed

Global illumination is implemented in at_least_one_bounce_radiance, which recursively accumulates light contributions across multiple bounces. When called with a ray and intersection, the function first returns immediately if r.depth == 0 (m=0, no bounces). Otherwise it computes one-bounce direct lighting via one_bounce_radiance — added unconditionally when isAccumBounces is true, or only at the deepest level (r.depth == 1) when false, so that unaccumulated renders isolate exactly one specific bounce.

To continue the path, we sample a new direction using isect.bsdf->sample_f (cosine-weighted hemisphere), construct a new ray from the hit point with min_t = EPS_F and depth = r.depth - 1, and recursively call at_least_one_bounce_radiance if the new ray hits something. The indirect contribution is: \[ L_{\text{indirect}} = \frac{f(\omega_o, \omega_i) \cdot L_{\text{recursive}} \cdot \cos\theta}{p(\omega_i) \cdot p_{\text{RR}}} \] Russian Roulette is applied for all bounces after the first indirect one: with continuation probability \(p_{\text{RR}} = 0.65\), each path is randomly terminated with probability 0.35. The first indirect bounce always proceeds (no RR), guaranteeing at least one indirect contribution when max_ray_depth > 1. The raytrace_pixel function initializes each camera ray's depth to max_ray_depth.

Direct vs. Indirect Illumination Only

Unaccumulated Bounces — CBbunny.dae (1024 spp, isAccumBounces=false)

The 2nd bounce (m=2) fills in the shadows cast by the area light and adds the characteristic red/blue color bleeding from the Cornell box walls onto the bunny — effects entirely absent in direct-only rasterization. The 3rd bounce (m=3) further softens the scene by illuminating areas that still appeared dark after two bounces, such as the underside of the bunny and the floor beneath it. These indirect contributions are impossible to achieve with rasterization without expensive approximations like ambient occlusion or screen-space reflections, which is why path-traced global illumination produces significantly more realistic results.

Accumulated Bounces — CBbunny.dae (1024 spp, isAccumBounces=true)

Comparing the two series side-by-side reveals what each additional bounce actually contributes. In the unaccumulated views, m=0 shows only the bright ceiling light with everything else black; m=1 adds direct illumination so the bunny and walls become visible, but shadows are hard and the scene looks flat; m=2 is noticeably dimmer than m=1 yet it provides the soft, colorful fill light — the red and blue wall colors begin bleeding onto the bunny and the floor shadow softens. By m=3 and beyond the unaccumulated images are very dark, contributing only subtle ambient fill that is hard to perceive in isolation. In the accumulated series the effect of stacking these bounces is clear: each increment from m=0 to m=2 produces a large visual jump — more illuminated surfaces, softer shadows, and color bleeding — while m=3 through m=5 change the image very little, meaning the energy from higher-order bounces is small relative to the total. This explains why m=5 and m=100 look essentially the same in practice: the first two or three indirect bounces capture nearly all of the scene's indirect illumination.
